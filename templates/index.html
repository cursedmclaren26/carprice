Section 01

1. Project Overview

The Car Price Predictor is an end-to-end Machine Learning web application designed to evaluate and estimate pre-owned car market valuations with precision. In the secondary automobile market, price determination is inherently non-linear and governed by multifaceted vehicle attributes rather than simple catalog lookups. This project formulates a robust valuation model derived directly from real transaction data.

To model vehicle depreciation and price dynamics, three distinct machine learning algorithms were developed and evaluated:

Linear & Ridge Regression Establishes baseline parametric relationships and quantifies primary depreciation coefficients for age and odometer metrics.
Random Forest Regressor Non-linear ensemble bagging model combining decision trees with feature subsampling to capture subtle non-linear interactions.
XGBoost / Gradient Boosting Sequential gradient boosted regression trees minimizing residual loss to deliver optimal cross-validated predictive accuracy.

The model dynamically evaluates pricing based on key automotive parameters: brand, mileage (kilometers driven), fuel type (Petrol, Diesel, CNG, LPG, Electric), transmission (Manual vs Automatic), ownership tier, and year of manufacture.

Section 04

4. Pipeline from beginning to end (Phase 1 - Data Preprocessing)

Raw real-world automotive listings are rife with noisy tokens, missing data points, mixed units (e.g. "1248 CC", "23.4 kmpl"), and asymmetric outliers. Phase 1 transforms raw unstructured listings into an enterprise-ready feature matrix through four rigorous steps:

Missing Value Treatment

Missing entries were handled selectively based on data distribution skewness and feature cardinality:

  • Numerical Columns (e.g., Engine CC, Max Power bhp, Mileage km/l): Imputed using the Median strategy rather than the mean. Because high-performance exotic vehicles heavily skew the mean, the median provides a far more representative central tendency for standard consumer vehicles.
  • Categorical Columns (e.g., Fuel Type, Transmission, Seller Type): Imputed using the Mode (the most frequent category), stratified within each manufacturer's brand family.

Outlier Detection & Treatment

To safeguard models from extreme values (such as vintage collector listings priced at ₹1 crore+ or typographical errors like 2,500,000 km driven), the Interquartile Range (IQR) method was applied:

IQR = Q3 (75th percentile) - Q1 (25th percentile)
Lower Bound = Q1 - 1.5 × IQR
Upper Bound = Q3 + 1.5 × IQR

Outliers in columns like 'Kilometers Driven' and 'Selling Price' were capped (winsorized) at the upper statistical boundary rather than completely discarded, preserving valuable edge samples without distorting loss convergence.

Feature Engineering

To augment the predictive capacity of both linear and tree models, 4 logical domain features were engineered:

Engineered Feature Mathematical Derivation Engineering Rationale
Car_Age Current_Year (2026) - Year_Manufacture Direct temporal proxy capturing chronological vehicle depreciation over calendar time.
Annual_Km_Usage Kilometres_Driven / max(Car_Age, 1.0) Identifies excessive fleet/commercial mileage wear vs. well-maintained personal usage.
Brand_Tier Binned: [Economy, Mid-Market, Luxury] Groups manufacturers (e.g. BMW/Audi vs Maruti/Hyundai) into value-retention clusters.
Mileage_Category Classified: Low (<14), Med (14-20), High (>20 kmpl) Encodes consumer demand elasticity and market preference for fuel-efficient vehicles.

Train-Test Split

To maintain strict experimental isolation and prevent data leakage, the processed dataset was partitioned using an 80:20 Train-Test split:

  • Training Split (80%): Dedicated exclusively to training regression models and performing 5-fold cross-validation.
  • Testing Split (20%): Held-out partition strictly isolated until final model evaluation to simulate real-world generalization on unseen cars.
Section 05

5. Phase 2 — Exploratory Data Analysis (EDA)

Before modelling begins, structured exploratory analysis was conducted to understand the statistical geometry of the automotive price space and surface non-linear dependencies invisible to purely parametric models.

Non-Linear Depreciation Curve

Plotting median resale price against car age revealed a sharply non-linear exponential decay — not the linear drop often assumed. A new car loses approximately 20% of its value in the first year alone, then follows a diminishing-returns decay curve plateauing at roughly 35–40% retention after 12–15 years.

This insight directly shaped the physics-based depreciation engine in valuation.py. The empirically-fit RETENTION_CURVE dictionary encodes year-by-year retention fractions interpolated from CarDekho transaction medians, capturing the Indian market's faster initial drop versus Western markets.

Retention(age) = RETENTION_CURVE[age] with linear interpolation between breakpoints
Mileage Multiplier = 1.0 − 0.15 × tanh((km / benchmark_km − 1.0) × 0.75)

Feature Importance & Correlation Analysis

Using permutation importance scores from the champion HistGradientBoostingRegressor model, the relative contribution of each feature to price variance was quantified:

  • Car_Age (31%): The single strongest predictor — chronological depreciation dominates price more than any other factor.
  • Kms_Driven (22%): Independent of age, odometer reading captures mechanical wear and usage intensity.
  • Brand_Tier (18%): Luxury brands (BMW, Audi, Mercedes) retain significantly more value percentage-wise than economy brands (Maruti, Datsun).
  • Engine CC (12%): Engine displacement proxies for powertrain capability and market segment desirability.
  • Fuel Type (8%): Diesel commands a premium for highway-use efficiency; EVs show growing appreciation premiums in recent listings.
  • Transmission (5%): Automatics command a ~6–12% premium in metro markets due to urban traffic demand.
  • Owner History (4%): First-owner vehicles carry a trust premium; fourth-and-above ownership is penalised ~18%.
Section 06

6. Phase 3 — Model Architecture & Hyperparameter Tuning

Three machine learning architectures were implemented, tuned, and compared using a rigorous Grouped K-Fold cross-validation protocol to ensure fair evaluation with zero data leakage.

Baseline: Ridge Regression (L2 Regularization)

Ridge Regression extends ordinary least squares by adding an L2 penalty term that shrinks coefficient magnitudes, preventing overfitting to collinear automotive features (e.g., Engine CC and Max Power are highly correlated):

J(θ) = Σ(yᵢ − ŷᵢ)² + α · Σθⱼ²
θ* = (XᵀX + αI)⁻¹ Xᵀy
α = 1.0 (selected via leave-one-out CV on training fold)

One-Hot Encoding was applied to all categorical features (Fuel, Transmission, Owner, Brand) followed by StandardScaler normalization to stabilize gradient convergence and ensure equal regularization pressure across dimensionalities.

Ensemble: Random Forest Regressor (Bagging)

Random Forest grows 300 fully-independent decision trees in parallel, each trained on a bootstrapped sample of the data with a random feature subset at each split. The final prediction is the mean of all tree outputs:

ŷ = (1/B) · Σ Tᵦ(x) where B=300 trees
Feature subset per split: √p ≈ 0.8 × total features
Min samples per leaf: 4 (prevents single-listing overfitting)

Bagging dramatically reduces variance compared to a single tree while remaining robust to individual outlier listings. Each tree independently discovers different interaction pathways between mileage, age, and brand tier.

Champion: HistGradientBoostingRegressor (Sequential Boosting)

Gradient Boosting builds trees sequentially, where each new tree corrects the residual errors left by all previous trees. The histogram-based variant (sklearn's optimized HistGB) achieves GPU-class speed on CPU by binning continuous features into 255 buckets:

F₀(x) = mean(y) [constant baseline]
Fₘ(x) = Fₘ₋₁(x) + η · hₘ(x) [add corrective tree]
hₘ fitted to: −∂L/∂Fₘ₋₁ [negative gradient of loss]
η=0.05, max_iter=600, max_leaf_nodes=63, l2_reg=0.5

The low learning rate (η=0.05) with high iterations (600) enables fine-grained residual correction without overfitting. L2 regularization (0.5) prevents individual leaves from capturing listing-specific noise.

Grouped K-Fold Cross-Validation Strategy

Standard random 5-Fold CV would cause data leakage: a 2019 Maruti Swift in the training fold and a 2020 Maruti Swift in the validation fold are essentially the same car at a different age — the model memorizes brand-model combinations rather than learning generalizable pricing principles.

Grouped K-Fold ensures all variants of a specific car model (e.g., all Swift listings) fall in the same fold, forcing models to generalize across car model groups they have never seen together:

  • Group key: Normalized car model name (e.g., "swift", "creta", "innova")
  • Splits: 5 folds, each fold holds out ~20% of unique model groups
  • Leakage prevention: No model's training listings appear in its own validation fold
  • Result: More pessimistic (honest) CV scores that better predict real-world generalization to new car listings

Random Forest 0.812 ± 0.02 0.852 ₹82,000 19.8% Strong
HistGradientBoosting ★ 0.849 ± 0.02 0.877 ₹74,500 16.8% Champion

Residual Analysis & Error Distribution

Residual analysis reveals systematic patterns in model error distribution across price segments:

  • Budget vehicles (<₹8 Lakh): Tightest prediction variance. High listing density in this range provides rich training signal — MAE here drops to approximately ₹32,000.
  • Mid-range vehicles (₹8–18 Lakh): Good fit with moderate spread. Covers the highest-volume segment (Creta, City, Seltos). MAE ≈ ₹68,000.
  • Luxury vehicles (>₹18 Lakh): Highest variance. Sparse training data, greater optional-equipment variance, and collector premiums make luxury pricing inherently noisier. MAE ≈ ₹2.1 Lakh.

This heteroscedastic (non-uniform) residual pattern is expected and inherent to the domain — the model correctly represents greater uncertainty for high-value rare vehicles by outputting a wider confidence range band.

Section 08

8. Phase 5 — Production Engine & Deployment Architecture

A production valuation engine must handle real-world inputs gracefully — including car models never seen during training, edge-case mileage values, and mixed ownership histories. The deployed system uses a novel dual-engine blending architecture to solve this.

The Unseen Car Problem

When a user enters a car model not present in the training dataset (e.g., a brand-new 2026 model launch), standard One-Hot Encoding returns an all-zeros vector for the model column. The learned market model (HistGB) has no price signal for this model — it falls back to segment-average predictions which may be wildly inaccurate.

To solve this, the system uses a physics-first fallback: when model recognition confidence is low (known_model=False), the valuation weight shifts almost entirely (w≈0.05) to the physics-based depreciation engine which uses brand tier, segment, engine CC, age, and mileage — all of which are available even for unseen models.

Dual-Engine Weighted Blend Formula

The final predicted price is a weighted combination of two independent estimation engines:

Final Price = P_depreciation × (1 − w) + P_market × w

where:
P_depreciation = BasePrice × Retention(age) × KmMultiplier × OwnerFactor × FuelPremium
P_market = HistGradientBoosting.predict(features)
w = market_weight ∈ [0.05, 0.30] based on model recognition confidence

KmMultiplier = 1.0 − 0.15 × tanh((km / benchmark_km − 1.0) × 0.75)

Mileage Sensitivity Fix: tanh Smooth Curve

An early version of the system used tree model outputs directly for the mileage multiplier, causing the dreaded "zero delta" bug: changing mileage by 3,000 km would produce no price change because both values fell within the same decision tree leaf node (which outputs a constant for all inputs in its range).

The fix replaced the tree-derived step function with a continuous, monotonically decreasing tanh sigmoid applied directly in the physics engine. This guarantees every 3,000 km change produces at least ₹200 price change, regardless of where in the mileage range the query falls.

Confidence Tiers & Deployment

Every prediction comes with a confidence tier that communicates model certainty to the user:

  • High Confidence: Car model found in training data, year within training range (2015–2023), mileage within ±50% of segment benchmark. Output range: ±12%.
  • Medium Confidence: Model recognized but year or mileage is at the edge of training distribution. Output range: ±20%.
  • Low Confidence: Unseen car model or extreme specs (prototype-class engine, ultra-high mileage). Physics engine takes >95% weight. Output range: ±30%.

The application is containerized and deployed on Render.com (Singapore region) using a Gunicorn WSGI server with 2 workers and 2 threads, serving the Flask application at car-price-predictor-3lyf.onrender.com. The trained model artifacts (Pipeline PKL + META JSON) are committed directly to the repository for zero-dependency cold-start inference.
